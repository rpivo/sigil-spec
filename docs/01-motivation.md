# Motivation

> Status: draft. Non-normative.

## The objective

To raise the cost of publishing audio or video with no accountable origin record.

Stated negatively, because the negative version is where most proposals in this area go
wrong: the objective is **not** to determine whether a given file was generated by AI. That
question cannot be answered reliably by any mechanism proposed here, and pursuing it produces
systems that overstate their own results.

The achievable objective is friction and accountability. Make honest provenance cheap to
assert, verifiable by anyone, and difficult to omit without the omission being visible. What
follows is why the existing systems do not achieve that.

## Metadata is stripped by ordinary work

C2PA binds a signed manifest into the file container. This is the most serious and most
widely adopted provenance system in existence, and the criticism that follows is narrow: the
binding lives in a layer that routine processing discards.

Any tool that re-encodes a file without C2PA awareness drops the manifest. This is not an
edge case in music and film. A recording is bounced, mastered, format converted, delivered
to a distributor, transcoded by a platform, and re-encoded for adaptive streaming. Consumer
platforms compound it: several major messaging and social services re-encode uploads and
silently remove credentials.

C2PA's answer is the soft binding, a watermark or fingerprint that lets a verifier
re-discover a manifest held in a remote repository. That is a reasonable design, and it
works. It also means verification requires a network call and a reachable, maintained
repository, and that the proof itself never travels with the file.

## Declaration is not verification

The DDEX AI disclosure standard, developed with Spotify and announced in September 2025,
gave the music industry something it genuinely lacked: a shared, granular vocabulary for AI
involvement, covering vocals, instrumentation, composition, post-production, and lyrics,
delivered through the pipeline that already moves music metadata.

It is self-reported. A rightsholder states what happened and the statement propagates. There
is no signature, no binding to the audio, and no way for a downstream party to check. It
records a claim rather than establishing one.

This is not a flaw in DDEX. Disclosure and verification are different problems, and DDEX
solved the one it set out to solve. But it means the music industry now has a well-defined
place to put an answer, and no mechanism for making that answer trustworthy.

## Detection is not a foundation

Statistical detection of AI-generated media is an active research area and an unreliable
basis for consequential decisions. False positives fall hardest on legitimate creators, and
detector performance degrades as generators improve. A provenance system should not depend
on winning that race.

Sigil takes the opposite approach: assert at creation time, verify cryptographically, and
stay silent about files that carry no assertion.

## Why now

Two regulations took effect on 2 August 2026: the EU AI Act's Article 50 transparency
obligations and the California AI Transparency Act, the latter requiring embedded
disclosures and a free public tool for surfacing provenance.

More consequentially, from 1 January 2027 large online platforms are required to detect and
surface standards-compliant provenance data, and are barred from stripping it.

That last obligation changes the economics. Platforms have historically had no reason to
preserve provenance data through transcode. Shortly they will be required to, which makes
this an unusually good moment to propose a carrier designed to survive that pipeline.
